Regulation Crypto Assets

SEC comment-letter tracker · File No. S7-2026-27 · Release Nos. 33-11434; 34-106150

In August 2026 the SEC proposed Regulation Crypto Assets, a new 17 CFR part 228 creating a tailored offering regime for investment contracts involving crypto assets. It would open two doors, and they are not alike.

The small door exempts raises of up to $5 million over four years. The issuer posts its disclosure on its own website; there are no financial statements, no audit and no licensed intermediary, and it may advertise to anyone. The larger door, modelled on Regulation A, allows $20 million a year at Tier 1 and $75 million at Tier 2 — but it is not simply a bigger exemption. It requires an offering statement on a new Form 1-CRYPTO, financial statements, and continuing reports on Forms 1-KC, 1-SC and 1-UC; ordinary investors may buy, capped at 10 percent of income or net worth. At Tier 2 those financial statements must be audited. At Tier 1 they need not be — a company may raise $20 million from retail investors with no assurance of any kind.

The proposal would also add a safe harbor under which an issuer's own certification ends the investment contract, and preempt State registration for both primary and secondary transactions. Comments close 2026-10-20. Read the proposing release (PDF) or browse the SEC comment file.

This tracker logs every comment letter as it arrives and records, for each provision a letter engages, both its stance on the Commission's proposal and the direction it pushes. The second axis is the point: on this docket opposition comes from commenters who want less constraint and from commenters who want more, and a single support-or-oppose scale cannot tell them apart. The Methodology box below explains the coding.

Methodology

How the coding works  the two axes, the three raters, and what is frozen

Every letter is read and, for each of the 17 provisions it engages, two values are recorded. Stance is Support, Oppose or Conditional relative to the Commission's proposal, where Support backs the proposal, Oppose means keep things as they are, and Conditional means yes with modifications. Direction is Loosen, Tighten, Clarify or as-proposed, and it is what makes this docket legible: on the Tier 2 cap, a request for $150 million and a request for $50 million are both Oppose, and only the direction column separates them.

Direction is a meaningful dial on thirteen provisions. On P1 it is dominated by Clarify, because most commenters want definitional precision rather than a wider or narrower perimeter. On P15 and P16 it does not apply at all; those carry their own closed code lists and never get a two-sided bar. GEN carries an assessed stance on the proposal as a whole and no direction, because Posture already summarises which way a letter pushes. Posture itself is computed only from the provisions where direction is a dial, and a letter that engages none of them is shown as n-a rather than left off the chart.

Classification uses three independent Claude raters with different reading postures, a Primary, a Literalist and a Skeptic. All three votes are stored and the majority is computed, never entered directly, so agreement can be measured and the corpus re-analysed under a stricter or looser rule. Every element stance carries a verbatim evidence quote from the letter, and every direction call carries a one-line rationale.

Every row records the rubric version it was coded under. The rubric was calibrated on two eight-letter pilot batches, frozen, and then reopened once — as v1.1 — with every letter already coded back-filled in the same pass, so no change accumulates as undocumented drift. It is frozen at v1.1 and the whole corpus carries that version. Across the calibration, rater disagreement fell from 34 percent to 5 percent on direction and from 13 percent to 5 percent on stance. The change log lives in the project's DECISIONS.md.

Source documents are archived at ingestion with a SHA-256 checksum, because SEC docket URLs move and documents get replaced. Letters are deduplicated by document identifier, never by display name. Numbering is chronological and stable: a late-posted letter takes the next free number rather than renumbering the corpus.

Two of the dials are marked outside Posture. P1 and P17 are scope and mechanics, where a Clarify ask is definitional work rather than a push in either direction, so counting them would put drafting notes on the same scale as policy asks. Below the dials sit the three things that are not dials at all: GEN, which is the letter’s read on the proposal as a whole, and P15 and P16, where an argument about the Commission’s authority or the quality of its cost estimate is not louder or quieter but one of a fixed set of positions.

Where the raters disagreed

Every provision is read three times, by raters with different postures — one reading in good faith and in context, one taking only what the letter says explicitly, and one discounting the framing to ask what the request would actually do. All three votes are kept and the label is the majority. This is where they parted company.

What each provision means

P1  Definitional scope

Definitional scope: crypto asset, covered investment contract, exclusivity prong, associated network/application, related person

Release pages 33-42 · Numbered questions 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11

This is the perimeter. A crypto asset is caught only for as long as a “covered investment contract” wraps it — the promise by a team to build something — and these definitions decide which assets, which networks and which related people fall inside it. Narrowing a definition does not tidy the rule, it removes a class of conduct from the regime altogether, which is why letters here read as arguments about reach rather than as drafting notes.

Coding note. Mostly definitional. A Clarify-heavy column is a finding about the element, not a coding defect. Related-person breadth is the one real dial inside it. SCOPE element. Direction follows the effect on regulatory reach - narrowing what is caught, or widening who may use the regime, is Loosen; conditioning entry is Tighten; a purely definitional ask with no change in reach is Clarify.

Coded so far: 3 Support · 1 Oppose · 13 Conditional

P2  Airdrops, staking and usage rewards

Airdrops, staking and validator rewards, usage distributions as covered transactions counting against the cap

Release pages 37, 76-79 · Numbered questions 43, 44, 46, 54

Tokens handed out rather than sold — airdrops, staking and validator rewards, distributions for using the network — still count against the offering cap. A project that gives away a large share of its supply can therefore exhaust its allowance without having raised the money, which is the practical objection commenters make.

Coding note. SCOPE element. Direction follows the effect on regulatory reach - narrowing what is caught, or widening who may use the regime, is Loosen; conditioning entry is Tighten; a purely definitional ask with no change in reach is Clarify.

Coded so far: 1 Support · 1 Oppose · 6 Conditional

P3  Principles-based vs prescriptive disclosuredial, by convention

Principles-based vs prescriptive disclosure: the ten Rule 103(b) topics, social-media consistency, risk-factor boilerplate

Release pages 49-67 · Numbered questions 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 33, 36, 100, 101, 102, 103

Rule 103(b) names ten topics an issuer must cover and leaves the depth to the issuer, in the way scaled disclosure works elsewhere. The argument is whether naming topics is enough or whether the Commission should prescribe form and content. Several letters want a different form of disclosure — machine-readable, versioned, evidence-backed — rather than more of it, and that is where this tracker’s raters most often disagree.

Coding note. Convention: more prescriptive = Tighten, because it removes issuer discretion. Says nothing about cost; the Commission concedes at pp. 265-266 that prescriptive could be cheaper.

Coded so far: 2 Support · 2 Oppose · 6 Conditional

P4  Where disclosure lives, and how it reaches investorsdial

Where disclosure lives and how it reaches investors: website-only vs EDGAR; delivery method unspecified

Release pages 66, 89-93 · Numbered questions 32, 59, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 88, 89, 90

Under the small door the issuer posts its disclosure on its own website. Nothing goes to EDGAR, and the proposal does not say how the disclosure must reach an investor before they buy. Commenters divide on whether a website the issuer controls is a filing system at all.

Coded so far: 0 Support · 1 Oppose · 5 Conditional

P5  Financial statements, audit and assurancedial

Financial statements, audit and assurance: US GAAP mandate, no Tier 1 assurance at up to $20M, GAAS vs PCAOB standards, no PCAOB registration, FASB deferral

Release pages 136-143 · Numbered questions 104, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 110, 111

Tier 2 financial statements must be audited. Tier 1’s need not be audited at all, so up to $20 million can be raised from ordinary investors with no assurance of any kind. Where an audit is required it may follow the AICPA’s standards rather than the PCAOB’s, and the firm need not be PCAOB-registered — so no inspection of the engagement and no PCAOB enforcement. The Commission also names a gap in the underlying accounting, on when a crypto asset goes on and comes off the balance sheet, and defers it to the FASB.

Coding note. Three tightening levers with their own questions: RFC 108 (PCAOB standards), RFC 110 (Tier 1 assurance), RFC 111 (PCAOB registration).

Coded so far: 0 Support · 3 Oppose · 3 Conditional

P6  Startup exemption calibrationdial

Startup exemption calibration: $5M cap, four-year runway, one-time-use limit including affiliates

Release pages 79-85 · Numbered questions 47, 51, 52, 53, 56, 57

The small door: $5 million over four years, once per issuer, counting affiliates. The questions are the size of the cap, whether four years is the right window, and whether a one-time limit can be enforced against a team that reorganises.

Coded so far: 2 Support · 4 Oppose · 4 Conditional

P7  Fundraising exemption calibrationdial

Fundraising exemption calibration: two tiers, $20M / $75M caps, affiliate sub-caps, 30% first-year secondary cap

Release pages 107-110 · Numbered questions 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 82, 83, 84

The larger door, modelled on Regulation A: $20 million a year at Tier 1 and $75 million at Tier 2, with sub-caps on affiliates and a 30 percent limit on secondary sales in the first year. Commenters argue the caps in both directions at once, which makes this the clearest place the direction axis earns its keep.

Coding note. The cleanest illustration of the two-axis need: $150M (CfPA) and $50M (Alt 9) are both Oppose.

Coded so far: 1 Support · 3 Oppose · 2 Conditional

P8  Retail access and investment limitsdial

Retail access and investment limits: 10%-of-income-or-net-worth cap on non-accredited purchasers, both tiers, no exchange-listing carveout

Release pages 115-118 · Numbered questions 55, 152

Ordinary investors may buy, capped at 10 percent of income or net worth. This is the proposal’s principal retail guardrail, and the Commission put a single numbered question to it while raising it in prose elsewhere. Thin engagement here is therefore as much a fact about how the release was written as about who has filed.

Coding note. Draws a single numbered question (RFC 152); the Commission raises it in prose at p. 118.

Coded so far: 0 Support · 3 Oppose · 0 Conditional

P9  Insider resaledial

Insider resale: covered investment contracts are not restricted securities; disclosure-only vs holding period vs milestone lockup

Release pages 60-61, 121-128 · Numbered questions 34, 35, 81

Tokens sold under either exemption are not restricted securities, so founders and early backers may sell immediately — no holding period, and public solicitation is allowed. The release concedes in its own footnote that insiders are rewarded for not finishing the project, because control retains its value, and cites research that offerings with insider lockups fail less often. It proposes disclosure only. Two industry commenters asked the Commission for lockups and were turned down.

Coding note. Coinbase and LeXpunK asked for lockups and were declined (pp. 122-124). Industry on the tightening side.

Coded so far: 1 Support · 1 Oppose · 2 Conditional

P10  Issuer eligibility and US nexus

Issuer eligibility and US nexus: entity-only requirement, majority-US officers, >50% US assets; whether a DAO can comply at all

Release pages 110-115 · Numbered questions 48, 49, 50, 60, 85, 86, 87, 97, 121

Who may use the regime: an entity, with a majority of US officers or more than half its assets in the United States. The question commenters press is whether a decentralised organisation with no legal entity can comply at all, which decides whether a whole class of projects is inside the regime or outside it.

Coding note. SCOPE element. Direction follows the effect on regulatory reach - narrowing what is caught, or widening who may use the regime, is Loosen; conditioning entry is Tighten; a purely definitional ask with no change in reach is Clarify.

Coded so far: 2 Support · 3 Oppose · 5 Conditional

P11  Bad-actor disqualificationdial

Bad-actor disqualification, including the grandfather for pre-effective-date events

Release pages 67-71 · Numbered questions 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42

The usual bar on offerings involving people with certain regulatory or criminal histories, carried over from the existing exemptions, with a grandfather for events before the rule takes effect.

Coded so far: 0 Support · 0 Oppose · 2 Conditional

P12  Ongoing reportingdial

Ongoing reporting: Forms 1-KC / 1-SC / 1-UC, extension to Tier 1, issuer-centric vs network-centric current-report triggers

Release pages 152-160 · Numbered questions 116, 117, 118, 119, 120, 122

After the offering: Form 1-KC annually, Form 1-SC semiannually and Form 1-UC for current events. The arguments are whether Tier 1 should report at all, and whether a current report should be triggered by something happening to the issuer or by something happening to the network.

Coded so far: 0 Support · 1 Oppose · 5 Conditional

P13  The safe harbor and the exit testdial, by convention

The safe harbor and the exit test: effort-and-intent vs decentralisation/functionality, fulfilment-only vs abandonment, self-certification, permanence, silence on antifraud

Release pages 162-170 · Numbered questions 62, 124, 125, 126, 127, 128, 129, 130, 131, 132, 133, 134, 135

The exit. The issuer files Form TR certifying that it has completed or permanently ceased the efforts it promised and does not intend to make new ones; on that filing the contract is deemed to have ended and the asset stops being a security. Permanently, with no unwind, no third-party verification and no deadline. The certification is supported by an analysis the issuer writes itself. This is the most consequential determination in the regime — the moment federal securities law stops applying to an asset — and it rests on an unaudited management assertion.

Coding note. Direction codes fine (adding a condition = Tighten) but stops predicting bloc. Alternative 11 is a tightening ask much of the industry wants. Read with the entity bucket.

Coded so far: 1 Support · 3 Oppose · 19 Conditional

P14  State preemptiondial

State preemption: every purchaser deemed qualified, both tiers, secondary transactions, preemption switching off with issuer compliance

Release pages 170-183 · Numbered questions 136, 137, 138, 139, 140, 141, 142, 143, 144

Fifty state securities regulators sit on top of the SEC. The proposal switches them off for these offerings and for later resales, using the statutory hook that lets the Commission preempt state law for sales to “qualified purchasers” and defining every purchaser as one. Preemption lapses if the issuer falls behind on its filings, and the release concedes a buyer in the secondary market may have no way to tell which state of the world they are in.

Coded so far: 4 Support · 2 Oppose · 4 Conditional

P15  Commission authority and the legislative questionclosed code list

Commission authority and the legislative question: missing authority citation for Rule 400, the 18(b)(3) reading and Lindeen, Rule 102 direct-to-final, EO 14215, defer-to-Congress

Release pages 48, 103, 163-176, 184 · Numbered questions 22

Whether the Commission may do this at all: the missing authority citation for the exit rule, the reading of Securities Act section 18(b)(3) that carries the preemption, and whether the question belongs to Congress instead. Recorded as a closed list of positions rather than a direction, because “more” and “less” do not describe an argument about power.

Coding note. Closed code list, not a dial: Authority challenged / Authority defended / Defer to Congress / Procedural objection.

Coded so far: 0 Support · 0 Oppose · 0 Conditional

P16  Adequacy of the economic analysisclosed code list

Adequacy of the economic analysis: $42.8M annualised cost against zero monetised benefit, the 636-issuer baseline, 1993-2017 data vintage, the 5%-25% illiquidity discount

Release pages 184-280 · Numbered questions 145, 146, 147, 148, 149, 150, 151, 153, 154

The Commission puts the annualised cost of the proposal at $42.8 million and the annualised benefit at zero, saying it is unable to estimate one. Commenters challenge that, the count of issuers the estimate rests on, and its use of data running from 1993 to 2017. Also a closed code list rather than a direction.

Coding note. Closed code list, not a dial: Costs understated / Benefits overstated / Baseline unreliable / Analysis adequate.

Coded so far: 0 Support · 0 Oppose · 0 Conditional

General provisions and mechanics: Rule 100 definitions and currency conversion, Rule 101(d) insignificant deviation, valuation standards for non-cash consideration

Alternatives the Commission considered and declined

A proposing release has to say what else the Commission thought about and chose not to do. This one lists 13, each in its own words — the italicised line under each title is the release’s description of the road not taken. They matter because a commenter asking for one of them is not making a new suggestion: they are asking the Commission to reverse a choice it has already made and defended, and for which the release has already committed to a reason. That is a harder thing to leave unanswered in an adopting release than an idea raised for the first time in a comment letter.

7 of the 13 have been endorsed by at least one letter so far. The one to watch is Alternative 11, on P13: an objective decentralisation-and-functionality test for the exit, in place of the issuer certifying its own intent. It is a tightening ask that much of the industry actively wants, because an objective test is a more reliable exit than a self-certification a court can later unpick — exactly the kind of alliance a plain support-or-oppose reading of this docket would miss.

This table counts the choice, because that is what a letter argues about. Endorsements are counted from the letter, not from whether it cites the alternative by number — almost none of them do.
